Smart Plant Monitoring System

De Anza College · E10 Introduction to Engineering · April–June 2025 · Six-person team

I proposed and led a six-person team project to build a plant-monitoring and automatic-watering prototype. An Arduino UNO R4 WiFi connected soil-moisture and temperature/humidity sensing, an LCD, indicator LEDs, and a reservoir pump to a Firebase-backed web dashboard.

Arduino R4 · Embedded programming · Firebase · Sensor testing · Team coordination

The problem

Water a plant when the soil reaches a dry threshold and make its moisture readings accessible remotely.

My contribution

I proposed the project, planned team tasks, created an initial sketch, and gave functional feedback on the enclosure design. I worked on sensor and LED programming alongside a teammate and implemented Firebase with another teammate. I also wrote pump-control code and contributed to the Wi-Fi/Firebase test documentation and Gantt chart. Teammates handled the HTML/CSS dashboard, detailed CAD modelling, and printing. The team tested dry, partly moist, and wet soil to establish reference readings.

How it worked

The controller compared the sensor reading with the chosen watering thresholds, switched on the reservoir pump when watering was needed, and stopped it when the target moisture level was detected. Wi-Fi connected the device to Firebase for live moisture data and a remote command that ran the pump for three seconds.

Integration challenge

The team addressed fluctuating moisture readings by averaging multiple samples. We isolated LCD and Wi-Fi issues with standalone test sketches, then tested cloud uploads with known values and checked their format and location directly in Firebase. Conflicting libraries and incompatible example code were the main integration obstacles; we researched alternatives and revised the examples until communication worked.

Result & reflection

The prototype demonstrated automatic watering, moisture and environmental sensing, LED feedback, and online data display, as documented in the team report. The report’s parts table lists a total of $80.71. Limitations included occasional Wi-Fi lag and no low-power indicator; future work included broader environment testing and improved power feedback. The prototype photo shows the printed enclosure, LCD, indicators, and laptop dashboard.

Subaru FA20 — Complete Engine Teardown & Rebuild

After the engine developed rod knock, I removed it from the car, completely disassembled it, and put it back together after replacing internal components and service parts. The rebuild included engine timing, gaskets, seals, lubrication, and fluids. I checked compression and returned the car to service, driving it for years before selling it.

Complete teardown & reassembly · Engine timing · Internal components · Compression testing

The problem

Address rod knock through an engine teardown and replacement of internal components.

My contribution

I handled engine removal, complete disassembly, and reassembly. I replaced components including the crankshaft, pistons, required bearings, seals, and gaskets; lubricated components during assembly; set the engine timing; and followed specified torque values and tightening sequences. I also handled the fluids needed to return the engine to service.

Specialist work

A machine shop handled cylinder-head resurfacing and testing. My role covered the complete engine teardown and rebuild, including component replacement, timing, reassembly, and the compression check.

Verification & outcome

The engine started and ran after the rebuild. Compression held as expected, and the car remained in use for years before I sold it, without significant issues reported from the rebuild.

What the work demonstrates
